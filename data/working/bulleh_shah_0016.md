# Literal Translation

Who am I?  Does anyone know?

In a mosque no worshipper,
nor a temple-frequenter,
I'm not pure, nor impure,
nor Moses, nor Pharaoh.

    Does anyone know?

What is good, what badness?
What is mirth, what sadness?
I'm not earth, water, fire,
nor the winds that blow.

    Does anyone know?

_Reference translation by Taufiq Rafat, "Bulleh Shah: A Selection" (Vanguard, 1982), p.69. COPYRIGHTED — private reference only; do NOT publish or use for training. Rafat renders the refrain "Bulleh, ki jaana main kaun" as "Does anyone know?" and reworks the second stanza's imagery._

# Tashreeh

This is Bulleh Shah's most famous poem, and its method is systematic self-negation — the mystic's via negativa, which the Indic tradition names neti neti, "not this, not this." The refrain, "what do I know of who I am?", is not a confession of confusion but a claim: the true self cannot be captured by any of the categories the world uses to fix identity, and the poem proves it by refusing them one after another. The refusals are ordered with care. First the religious binaries — "not a believer in the mosque, nor a keeper of unbelief's rites" — rejecting both islām and kufr as labels, and with them the whole sectarian apparatus that sorts souls into believer and infidel.

The negations then widen. "Not pure among the pure, nor defiled" dissolves the purity/pollution axis on which so much ritual law — and caste — depends; "not Moses, nor Pharaoh" refuses even the archetypes of prophet and tyrant, the poles of sacred history, so that the self is neither the saved nor the damned exemplar. The second stanza turns from the religious to the affective and the cosmological: "neither wedding-joy nor grief" sets the self outside the pairs of emotion, and "not water, earth, fire, or wind" sets it outside the chār ʿanāṣir, the four elements of which all bodies are composed. That last move is decisive — having refused every social, religious, and moral label, Bulleh Shah refuses even material, elemental existence: the self is not the body, not made of the stuff of the created world at all.

The cumulative logic is apophatic and monist. By stripping away every predicate — creed, purity, sacred role, emotion, element — the poem points to a self that is none of these because at its ground it is identical with the One that no category contains: the rūḥ or ātman that is a spark of the undivided Real, definable only by negation. This is the same intuition as the Upaniṣadic neti neti and as the Sufi teaching that the realized self, in fanāʾ, has shed every attribute (ṣifa) that individuates it, leaving only the divine. The refrain's "I do not know who I am" is thus the highest knowing in disguise: to be unclassifiable is to have escaped the false self entirely.

Historically the refusal of the mosque/unbelief and pure/impure binaries carries a sharp social charge in a Punjab riven by sect and caste — a manifesto against every label that divides human beings, of a piece with "Chal Bulleh" (0008) and the "one point" kafi (0013). A note for the corpus: Rafat prints only these two opening stanzas, but the poem is much longer in the standard tradition (continuing "not Arab nor Lahori… not Hindu nor Turk…"), so this entry should be marked an excerpt and the fuller text considered for inclusion. # Key Terms

- neti neti: "not this, not this" — the method of self-negation the poem enacts; do_not_flatten_to simple denial.
- momin / kufr: believer / unbelief — the religious binary the poem refuses; do_not_flatten_to a personal profession of doubt.
- pāk / palīt: pure / defiled — the purity-pollution axis of ritual law and caste, here dissolved; do_not_flatten_to hygiene.
- Moosa / Firaun: Moses / Pharaoh — the archetypes of prophet and tyrant, both refused; do_not_flatten_to mere historical figures.
- chār ʿanāṣir: the four elements (water, earth, fire, wind) of which bodies are made — refused, so that the self is not the body; do_not_flatten_to literal chemistry.
