# Original

اب تو جاگ مسافر پیارے
رین گئی، لتکے سب تارے
آ واگوں سرائیں ڈیرے
ساتھ تیار مسافر تیرے
اجے نہ سنیوں گونج نقارے
اب تو جاگ مسافر پیارے
گرے اج گرنی دا ویرا
مڑ نہ ہوسی آون تیرا
ساتھی چلو چل پکارے
اب تو جاگ مسافر پیارے
موتی، چونی، پارس پاسے
پاس سمندر، مرو پیاسے
کھول اکھیں، اٹھ بوہ بیکارے
اب تو جاگ مسافر پیارے

# Reference Translation

The last stars have been scrubbed from the skies.
Wayfarer, arise!
    The camp is astir, you are too slow,
    your companions are ready to go.
    Are you deaf to the clangour and cries?
Wayfarer, arise!
    Up, up, from too much fuss refrain,
    for you will not come this way again;
    the rest are saddled up, unwise.
Wayfarer, arise!
    You check each article you own
    when none will help. So much to be done
    and you laze in bed with tight-shut eyes.
Wayfarer, arise!

_Reference translation by Taufiq Rafat, "Bulleh Shah: A Selection" (Vanguard, 1982), p.39. COPYRIGHTED — private reference only; do NOT publish or use for training._

# Tashreeh

The poem belongs to the widespread Sufi genre of the "awakening" or caravan-song, and its whole force comes from one sustained allegory: the sleeping musafir (traveller) is the unawakened soul, sleep is heedlessness (ghafla), and the night that "has passed" with its "spent stars" is the lifetime draining toward dawn — death. The refrain, tolling after each stanza, is alarm rather than comfort: jāg, "wake," is the imperative of the whole path, the demand to rouse from the sleep of the ego before the light comes up. The world is figured as a sarāī, a caravanserai — a wayfarers' inn where one lodges a single night and never settles — one of the tradition's central emblems for the transience of dunya.

The middle stanza draws its urgency from the material life of the caravan: the companions are "saddled and ready," the naqqāra — the kettle-drum beaten to signal a caravan's departure, and in royal usage the drum of authority — is already sounding, and the cry goes up, "move on, move on." The implication is communal and pressing: others are completing the journey while the sleeper lingers, and the soul risks being left behind by the very procession of the dying. The flat announcement that "there will be no coming back" (muṛ na hosī āvan) states the irreversibility that gives the alarm its weight — this caravan sets out once.

The final stanza turns on attachment and, more sharply, on self-imposed blindness. The sleeper "checks each article he owns" at the very moment when none of it will help — the standard reminder that nothing material accompanies the soul — and then comes the poem's finest figure: "the ocean is beside you, yet you die of thirst." The paradox condenses the whole tragedy of ghafla: the Water of Life, the Beloved, the Real, is nearer than near (the indwelling God of the other kafis), and the soul perishes of a thirst it need not suffer, for want only of opening its eyes. "Pearls, silver, the philosopher's stone lie at hand" works the same way — the alchemical pāras that turns base metal to gold (transforming grace, the murshid's touch) is within reach and ignored. The cure is named as the barest act, "open your eyes, rise": awakening is not acquisition but the lifting of a blindness one has imposed on oneself. # Key Terms

- musafir: the traveller — the soul passing through the world, never a resident; do_not_flatten_to an ordinary traveller or tourist.
- sarāī: caravanserai, the wayfarers' inn — the world (dunya) as a one-night lodging, not a home; do_not_flatten_to a literal building.
- naqqāra: the kettle-drum signalling a caravan's departure (and royal authority) — here the summons of death and the path; do_not_flatten_to generic drums.
- ghafla: heedlessness, the sleep of the unawakened soul — the state the refrain commands one to break; do_not_flatten_to mere drowsiness.
- pāras: the philosopher's stone / alchemical touchstone — transforming grace lying unused within reach; do_not_flatten_to a mere jewel.
