# Original

کدی آ مِل یار پیاریا

دُور و دُور اساتھوں گیئوں
اصلاتے جا کے بہ رہیئوں
کیوں قصور وِساریا
کدی آ مِل یار پیاریا

در کھُلّا حَشر عذاب دا
بُرا حال ہویا پنجاب دا
وچ ہاویلے دوزخ ساڑیا
کدی آ مِل یار پیاریا

کدیں آویں وَڈھ پرواریا
تیرے دُکھاں سانوں ماریا
مُنہ بارھویں صدی پلساریا
کدی آ مِل یار پیاریا

بُلھا! شوہ میرے گھر آوسی
میری بلدی بھاہ بُجھاوسی
عنایت دَم دَم نال چتاریا
کدی آ مِل یار پیاریا

# Literal Translation

Come see me once in a while.

You are gone, friend, far away
beyond my reach. Many a day
Kasur has missed your smile.
    Come see me once in while.

Now hell has opened its bogs.
Punjab has gone to the dogs.
This hell is the nethermost hell.
    Come see me once in a while.

A brief visit now and then
will give some ease to my pain.
This century cramps our style.
    Come see me once in a while.

The flames engulfing my house,
Bulleh, only love can douse.
Inayat calls up the aisle
    Come see me once in a while.

_Reference translation by Taufiq Rafat, "Bulleh Shah: A Selection" (Vanguard, 1982), p.129. COPYRIGHTED — private reference only; do NOT publish or use for training. Rafat keeps "Kasur" and "Inayat" but generalises "the twelfth century" to "This century cramps our style."_

# Tashreeh

This is a rare and precious poem in the corpus: a topical, historically anchored lament that fuses the mystic's cry for the absent Beloved with an actual cry over the ruin of eighteenth-century Punjab. The refrain — "come, meet me sometime, beloved friend (yār pyāriyā)" — is the lover's and disciple's plea to the withdrawn Beloved or murshid, but the second and third stanzas ground that longing in concrete catastrophe: "the door of doomsday-torment (ḥashar, ʿazāb) has swung open; Punjab has fallen into a wretched state (burā hāl hoyā Punjab dā); within these halls a hell burns." Bulleh Shah lived through the collapse of Mughal order — the invasions of Nadir Shah and the Afghans, the sack of the Punjab, the chaos of the mid-eighteenth century — and here the standard eschatological vocabulary of doomsday and hell is turned to describe a real devastation: the province itself has become a burning hell.

The dating is explicit and striking: "the twelfth century (bārhvīṅ sadī) has bared its ugly face" — the twelfth century of the Hijra (roughly 1688–1785 CE), Bulleh Shah's own era. It is unusual for a mystic lyric to name its historical moment so directly, and it marks this kafi as a witness to the poet's times as well as his soul. The opening line's "why have you forgotten Kasur (Qasoor)?" carries the well-known pun — Qasoor is Bulleh Shah's own town, but qasoor also means "fault/sin," so the reproach hovers between "why have you forsaken my city?" and "what was our fault, that you left us?" — folding the historical desolation into the lover's sense of abandonment by the Beloved.

The final stanza turns from lament to faith and names the source of hope: "the Beloved (shoh) will surely come to my house and quench the fire that blazes within me; with every breath (dam dam) I call Inayat to mind." The "fire" is at once the literal burning of ravaged Punjab and the fire of firāq, the inward burning of separation, and only the Beloved's coming can douse it. The closing devotion — "with every breath I remember Inayat" — names the murshid Shah Inayat as the constant object of the poet's breath-by-breath remembrance (a description of continuous dhikr), so that the historical, the erotic-mystical, and the discipular strands all resolve into the one act of remembering the master through whom the Beloved comes. The poem is thus doubly valuable — as devotion and as a dated eyewitness lament for a broken Punjab. # Key Terms

- Qasoor: Kasur, Bulleh Shah's own town — punning on qasoor, "fault/sin," so the reproach means both "why forsake my city?" and "what was our fault?"; do_not_flatten_to only the place-name.
- ḥashar / ʿazāb: doomsday and its torment — eschatological vocabulary here describing a real historical devastation of Punjab; do_not_flatten_to abstract theology.
- bārhvīṅ sadī: the twelfth century of the Hijra (c. 1688–1785 CE) — the poet naming his own violent age; do_not_flatten_to a vague "this century."
- shoh: the Beloved (and the murshid) whose coming will quench the fire; do_not_flatten_to a human friend.
- Inayat: Shah Inayat, the murshid, remembered "with every breath" (continuous dhikr); do_not_flatten_to a passing mention.
