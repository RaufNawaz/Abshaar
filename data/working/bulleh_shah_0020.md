# Original

تیرے عشق نچائیاں

تیرے عشق نچائیاں کر کے تھیّا تھیّا!
تیرے عشق نے ڈیرا میرے اندر کیتا
بھر کے زہر پیالہ میں تاں آپے پیتا
جھب سے بوہڑیں وے طبیبا، نہیں تے میں مر گیّا
تیرے عشق نچائیاں کر کے تھیّا تھیّا!

چھپ گیا وے سورج، باہر رہ گئی آ لالی
وے میں صدقے ہوواں، دیویں مُڑ جے وِکھالی
پیرا! میں بُھل گیّاں تیرے، نال نہ گیّا
تیرے عشق نچائیاں کر کے تھیّا تھیّا!

اِس عشقے دے کولوں مینوں ہٹک نہ مائے
لاہو جاندڑے بیڑے، کیہڑا موڑ لِیائے
میری عقل جو بُھلّی، نال محانیاں دے گیّا
تیرے عشق نچائیاں کر کے تھیّا تھیّا!

# Transliteration

Tere ishq nachāiyān

Tere ishq ne ḍera mere andar kīta
Bhar ke zahr piyāla main tān āpe pīta
Jhab se bohṛīn ve tabība, nahīn te main mar giya
Tere ishq nachāiyān kar ke thaiyya thaiyya!

Chhup gaya ve sūraj, bāhar reh gayi ā lāli
Ve main sadqe hovān, deven muṛ je vikhāli
Pīra! main bhul giyān tere, nāl na giya
Tere ishq nachāiyān kar ke thaiyya thaiyya!

Is ishqe de kolon mainu haṭak na māe
Lāhu jāndṛe beṛe, kehṛa moṛ liāe
Meri aqal jo bhulli, nāl mahāniyān de giya
Tere ishq nachāiyān kar ke thaiyya thaiyya!

# Literal Translation

What a dance your love has led me

    since within me it opened its shop
    and I drank from its poisoned cup.
    Come to my help, apothecary.
        What a dance your love has led me!

    The sun has set; some redness remains.
    Unveil your face but once again
    to the simpleton who set you free.
        What a dance your love has led me!

    I would not be denied. Now the boat
    is in the whirlpool, barely afloat.
    I chose to stay on, foolishly.
        What a dance your love has led me!

_Reference translation by Taufiq Rafat, "Bulleh Shah: A Selection" (Vanguard, 1982), p.79. COPYRIGHTED — private reference only; do NOT publish or use for training. Rafat renders "ḍera keeta" as "opened its shop" and the third stanza's boat/boatmen imagery freely._

# Tashreeh

One of the most sung of all Bulleh Shah's kafis, and its governing figure is dance: "your love has set me dancing — thaiyya thaiyya," where thaiyya thaiyya names the actual beats of the dance, so the poem enacts the loss of self-possession it describes — love does not persuade the lover, it moves her body against her will. The dance is the outward sign of wajd, ecstatic absorption, and of the scandal that attaches to it: to dance in the street is to have abandoned the composure respectable piety demands, and the poem embraces precisely that exposure.

The first stanza gives love's violence two images. It has "pitched its camp inside me" (ḍerā keetā) — love as an occupying force that has taken up residence and dispossessed the self of its own house — and the drinking of "the cup of poison" (zahr dā piyālā) which "I drank myself." The self-administered poison is the crucial detail: the lover is complicit in her own undoing, and the cup recalls the Sufi motif of love as a lethal draught (with the shadow of Hallaj behind it). Hence the cry to the "healer" (ṭabīb), the physician who alone can cure the illness he caused — the standard figure for the murshid, or the Beloved himself, as the sole remedy for the wound of love.

The second stanza's "the sun has set, only a redness lingers" is hope fading to an afterglow — the light of the Beloved's presence withdrawn, a trace left on the horizon — and "turn and show your face once more" is again the longing for dīdār. The address to the pīr (master) and the confession "I lost my way, I could not keep pace with you" cast the drama as discipleship failed and yearning to be restored. The third stanza completes the surrender: "do not hold me back, O mother" refuses the family/social voice (as in the caste poems) that tries to restrain the lover, and the closing image is the sinking boat and, most tellingly, "my reason (ʿaql), gone astray, has drifted off with the boatmen."

That last line is the poem's philosophical core. ʿAql — discursive reason, the faculty of prudence and self-preservation — has abandoned ship, because love and reason cannot cohabit and the price of ʿishq is the loss of ʿaql. This is the classic Sufi ranking of ʿishq over ʿaql (as in Rumi and ʿAttar): reason calculates, hesitates, and preserves the self, while love capsizes the boat and drowns the calculating self entirely. The poem thus moves from the involuntary dance, through the self-poisoning and the failed discipleship, to the final shipwreck of the rational ego — a complete anatomy of annihilation by love. # Key Terms

- ishq: overpowering love — here a violent, dispossessing force, not sentiment; do_not_flatten_to affection.
- thaiyya thaiyya: the named beats/steps of the dance love compels — the enactment of ecstatic self-loss (wajd); do_not_flatten_to nonsense syllables.
- ḍerā keetā: "pitched camp / made a dwelling" — love as an occupying force taking over the self's house; do_not_flatten_to a casual arrival.
- zahr dā piyālā: the "cup of poison" the lover drinks herself — love as a lethal, self-administered draught; do_not_flatten_to literal poison.
- ṭabīb: the healer/physician — the murshid or Beloved who alone can cure the wound he caused; do_not_flatten_to an ordinary doctor.
- ʿaql: discursive reason, prudence, self-preservation — that which love shipwrecks; do_not_flatten_to intelligence in general.
