# Original

الف اللہ دل رتّا میرا
مینوں ب دی خبر نہ کائی
ب پڑھیاں کجھ سمجھ نہ آوے
الف دی لذت آئی
ع تے غ دا فرق نہ جاناں
ایہ گل الف سمجھائی
بلھیا! قول الف دے پُرے
جہڑے دل دی کرن صفائی

# Reference Translation

A for Allah who has my heart,
I have no knowledge of B,
nor do I know what it means,
while A savours sweet to me.
I can't tell between O and Q,
it makes me dither and delve;
Bulleh, look after the first,
the rest will take care of itself.

# Tashreeh

This is the most famous specimen of Bulleh Shah's ḥurūfī (letter-mystical) manner, and its argument is built entirely on the Perso-Arabic alphabet as a map of knowledge. Alif — the first letter, a single vertical stroke, numerically one — is the cipher of Allah and of tawḥīd, the undivided One; and "Alif has dyed my heart" (ratta) uses the dyer's image, the fast colour of love, for a heart so saturated by the One that no second thing shows in it. To hold Alif is to hold everything, which is why the poem refuses "be," the second letter: be stands for the whole downward multiplication of letters — that is, for ʿilm, acquired discursive learning, the grammar and theology of the madrasa. "I have no knowledge of be" is therefore not a confession of ignorance but a deliberate ranking; the lover who has tasted the One (lazzat, the "savour" of Alif) has lost the appetite for the catalogue of the many.

The ain/ghain figure turns the critique to satire. The two letters are graphically identical but for a single dot (nuqta), yet on that dot the learned build endless distinctions; "I cannot tell ain from ghain" is a boast, not a lapse — the gnostic has burned past the level at which such hairline differences signify. This is the antinomian, anti-scholastic edge that runs through the whole corpus and set Bulleh Shah against qazi and pandit alike: letter-learning that divides and disputes is exactly what love of the One dissolves. The nuqta is itself a tradition — the Sufi commonplace that the whole Qur'an is folded into the Fatiha, the Fatiha into the bismillah, the bismillah into the letter ba, and the ba into the single dot beneath it: all of revelation compressed to a point.

The close names the practical upshot: "hold fast the word of Alif that scours the heart clean." The verb of cleansing (safāī) reframes the alphabet as a discipline rather than a curriculum — Alif is not a datum to be learned but a purgative to be undergone, so that knowledge here is the transformation of the knower (taṣfiya, purification of the heart, the precondition of maʿrifa). Two philological cautions for review: "ratta" (dyed/absorbed) and the manuscript's bare-alif shorthand are flagged uncertain, and this Rafat opening ("Alif Allah dil ratta mera") must be kept distinct from the more famous "Alif Allah chambe di booti" often attributed to Bulleh Shah — a different poem with which it is easily conflated.

# Key Terms

- Alif: the first letter — a single stroke, the numeral one — as cipher of Allah and of tawḥīd, the undivided One; do_not_flatten_to a mere alphabet letter.
- be: the second letter, standing for ʿilm, discursive book-learning and the multiplication of forms; do_not_flatten_to the literal letter B.
- ratta: dyed, colour-fast, absorbed — the heart saturated by the One as cloth takes fast dye; do_not_flatten_to simply "in love."
- nuqta: the dot distinguishing ain from ghain — the hairline on which scholastic dispute turns, and the point in which all revelation is folded; do_not_flatten_to a punctuation mark.
- lazzat: the taste or savour of the One, experiential knowing; do_not_flatten_to ordinary pleasure.
