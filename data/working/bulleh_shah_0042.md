# Original

کیسی توبہ

کیسی توبہ ہے ایہ توبہ، ایسی توبہ نہ کر یار

مُونہوں توبہ، دِلوں نہ کردا، اِس توبہ تھِیں ترک نہ پھڑدا
کِس غفلت نے پایو پردا، تینوں بخشے کیوں غفار

سانوِیں دے کے لَؤں سوائے، ڈُہڑیاں اُتّے بازی لائے
مُسلمانی اوہ کِتّھوں پائے، جس دا ہووے ایہ کِردار

جِت نہ جانا اُتّے جاویں، حتیٰ بیگانہ ٹھمکر کھاویں
ٹھوڑ کِتاباں سِر تے چاویں، ہووے کِہ تیرا اعتبار

ظالِم ظُلموں ناہیں ڈردے، اپنی کیتیوں آپے مردے
ناہیں خوف خُدا دا کردے، ایتّے اُتّے ہوون خوار

کیسی توبہ ہے ایہ توبہ، ایسی نہ کر یار

# Reference Translation

What sort of penitence is this?

That sits on the lips, but has no place in the heart,
and yet expects to be granted bliss;

whose name is Muslim, but whose furtive eyes
look for lacunas; whose ambitions hiss;

whose heart is absent when the hand is placed
on the Holy Book in solemn promise.

O ruthless ones, the trap you set for others,
is set for you. Be sure it will not miss.

What sort of penitence is this?

_Reference translation by Taufiq Rafat, "Bulleh Shah: A Selection" (Vanguard, 1982), p.139. COPYRIGHTED — private reference only; do NOT publish or use for training._

# Tashreeh

A satire of counterfeit repentance, and one of Bulleh Shah's sharpest attacks on the gap between religious form and inner reality. The refrain's exasperated question — kaisī tauba hai eh tauba, "what kind of repentance is this?" — targets the tauba (repentance, the turning-back from sin) performed with the mouth but not the heart: "repentance on the lips, not from the heart; from such repentance he gives up nothing (tark)." The theological sting follows at once — "why should the Forgiver (al-Ghaffār) forgive you?" — since forgiveness presupposes a real turning, and the Ghaffār, however boundlessly forgiving, is not mocked by a repentance that renounces nothing. The premise is that God reads the heart, not the formula: a tauba without inner change is not merely useless but an affront to the Forgiver.

The middle stanzas (textually the most damaged, flagged HIGH uncertainty) extend the critique from the penitent to the hypocrite at large — one who "gives little and takes more," gambles, and yet lays claim to Islam though "his conduct (kirdār) is such as this." The recurrent Bulleh Shah principle is that Musalmānī, true Muslim-ness, is a matter of conduct and not profession: one cannot "attain Islam" while behaving as its opposite. The image of "setting the holy books on the head to swear" targets the hypocrite's oath — the solemn gesture of placing the Qur'an on one's head to guarantee truthfulness, performed by one in whom "no trust (aitbār) can rest"; the sacred oath is voided by the swearer's falsity.

The final stanza broadens into a warning against the tyrant (ẓālim): "the tyrants have no fear of their tyranny; they die by their own doings (apnī keetioṅ āpe marde); they hold no fear of God — here and hereafter (ette utte) they are brought to disgrace." The moral order is self-executing — the tyrant needs no external punishment because "he dies by his own deeds"; cruelty recoils on the cruel, the trap set for others closing on the one who set it (as Rafat's rendering makes explicit). The poem thus moves from the false penitent, through the hypocrite who games religious forms, to the fearless tyrant, all united by the charge of a religion of surface without substance, and all answered by the single principle that God and the moral order see through the form to the deed. (Stanzas 2–3 are HIGH uncertainty; the refrain, the "lips not heart" line, the Ghaffār line, the oath-on-the-head image, and the tyrant stanza are clear.)

# Key Terms

- tauba: repentance, the turning-back from sin — here the hollow, lip-only version the poem condemns; do_not_flatten_to a mere apology.
- Ghaffār: "the Forgiver," a divine name — not to be mocked by a repentance that renounces nothing; do_not_flatten_to a generic "God."
